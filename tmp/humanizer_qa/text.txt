A B S T R A C T
Class imbalance leaves a classiﬁer with relatively few examples of the minority class. Re-
sampling can improve its representation, but may also introduce noise or remove useful
information. We investigate Coverage-Aware Safe-Border SMOTE (CASB), which distributes
a ﬁxed generation budget across overlapping pools of all minority observations, supported
borderline observations and sparse minority observations. The four variants use conditionally
capped interpolation, unrestricted interpolation, unrestricted interpolation followed by majority-
only edited nearest neighbours, and source duplication. We evaluated them on 44 binary KEEL
tasks using the ﬁve supplied folds, two seeds and training-only ratio selection, with SVM, KNN,
random forest and logistic regression as classiﬁers. Forty tasks support complete comparisons
with SMOTE, Borderline-SMOTE, random oversampling and ADASYN; ADASYN failed in
52 evaluations across the four classiﬁers. For random forest, the retrospective CASB family
exceeded the four core baselines on G-mean in 28 of 40 tasks and on F1 in 26, although the
selected variant could diﬀer by metric and task. Some ﬁxed-variant gains remained signiﬁcant
after classiﬁer-speciﬁc Holm correction, alongside losses in precision. No paired Wilcoxon
comparison survived correction in the source-family sensitivity analyses. A separate Gaussian
overlap experiment and audited generation examples examined the mechanisms behind these
outcomes. The results support a reproducible resampling design whose beneﬁts depend on the
classiﬁer and metric. Matched cleaner ablations and evaluation on independent source families
are needed before broader claims of superiority can be made.
1. Introduction
In supervised classiﬁcation, a model learns the relationship between input features and class labels. When one
class is much smaller than another, its observations may be sparse or isolated, including in regions shared with the
majority class. A dataset with 95 percent class A observations and 5 percent class B observations illustrates the
problem: predicting class A for every case gives 95% accuracy and zero minority recall. Accuracy alone therefore
gives a misleading account of minority detection.
Oversampling increases the minority class representation in the training set. Random oversampling (ROS)
duplicates existing minority observations, while SMOTE creates a point between a minority source and one of its
minority neighbours (Chawla, Bowyer, Hall and Kegelmeyer, 2002). Both can change the learned decision boundary,
but the added observations are not independent evidence. A synthetic point inherits its source label even when the line
between its endpoints crosses an overlapping region or connects disconnected components. Duplication avoids this
geometric assumption, although it increases the inﬂuence of the observations being copied.
We study how to distribute a ﬁxed number of additions among diﬀerent minority observations. CASB draws sources
from three overlapping pools: coverage contains all minority observations; border contains observations in locally
mixed neighbourhoods with minority support; and rare contains sparse observations with minority support. Original
CASB limits some interpolation steps according to the distance from the source to the nearest observed majority point.
The other variants remove these limits, clean majority observations after generation, or duplicate selected sources.
Comparing these conﬁgurations helps distinguish the roles of source allocation, generation and subsequent cleaning.

2. Literature review
2.1. Random repetition, interpolation and diﬃcult regions
Random oversampling increases the weight of minority observations by repeating them. It provides a useful
baseline because it makes no assumptions about where a new minority point should lie. Its limitation is that it adds
no new feature values and can amplify an incorrect label through repeated copies. Despite its simplicity, ROS can
outperform more elaborate methods and remains an important comparator.
SMOTE generates minority samples by interpolating between nearby minority observations (Chawla et al., 2002).
The generator is simple, but basic SMOTE does not use surrounding majority labels to constrain the selected segment.
Even a short segment can cross a majority region when its endpoints lie on opposite sides of that region.
Borderline-SMOTE concentrates generation around minority observations identiﬁed as being in danger from their
local labels (Han, Wang and Mao, 2005). This directs additions towards diﬃcult boundaries instead of distributing them
uniformly. We use borderline-1. Its source-selection rule excludes other minority observations from direct allocation;
whether broader allocation is preferable remains an empirical question. CASB reserves part of its budget for the all-
minority pool to examine this tradeoﬀ.
ADASYN assigns more additions to minority observations with greater local learning diﬃculty (He, Bai, Garcia
and Li, 2008). Its allocation adapts to the data, whereas CASB uses ﬁxed branch weights. A high local majority fraction
can indicate either a useful boundary observation or an unreliable minority label; the count alone does not distinguish
them. The ADASYN failures in this study reﬂect feasibility problems in the executed procedure. We exclude incomplete
tasks from every method in the matched comparison.
2.2. Safety, density and minority instance types
Safe-Level-SMOTE uses local minority support to modify interpolation (Bunkhumpornpat, Sinapiromsaran and
Lursinsap, 2009). Geometric SMOTE samples from a conﬁgurable geometric region rather than the original SMOTE
line segment (Douzas and Bacao, 2019). CASB also uses local information, but limits displacement according to

the allocated branch and nearest-majority distance. Some coverage steps remain unrestricted. A distance limit cannot
guarantee the label of a generated point, especially when the source label is wrong.
Density-based methods use structure within the minority class. DBSMOTE uses density information to generate
points (Bunkhumpornpat, Sinapiromsaran and Lursinsap, 2012), and K-means/SMOTE allocates additions using clus-
ters (Last, Douzas and Bacao, 2017). CASB uses a simpler sparsity measure: the distance to the third nearest minority
neighbour, thresholded at the 75th percentile. It does not ﬁt clusters or identify veriﬁed minority subpopulations. This
distance also depends on the feature representation and may become less informative in high-dimensional spaces.
Minority-instance classiﬁcation is a close predecessor to CASB’s source selection. Napierala and Stefanowski
(2016) distinguish borderline, rare and outlier observations using local class structure, while Skryjomski and Krawczyk
(2017) examine how minority-instance types aﬀect SMOTE. Local Neighborhood Encodings (LNE) optimize resam-
pling rates for neighbourhood types using both oversampling and undersampling (Koziarski and Woźniak, 2024).
CASB combines overlapping pools with ﬁxed weights, so its contribution does not lie in the general idea of selecting
minority-instance types. LNE is not included in this benchmark, and the results cannot establish an advantage over it.
2.3. Cleaning and uncertainty-guided frameworks
Edited nearest neighbours (ENN) removes observations whose labels disagree with their neighbours. We use the
imbalanced-learn implementation, with explicit choices of the targeted class and voting rule (cite). Cleaning after
generation can alter the training set substantially because synthetic observations participate in the neighbour votes.
CASB-edited uses an established cleaner and makes no claim to a new ENN rule.
A general SMOTE-ENN comparison does not provide a matched control for CASB-edited. Our cleaner targets the
majority class and uses three-neighbour mode voting; other hybrid implementations can target diﬀerent classes or use
diﬀerent settings. The current comparison therefore cannot isolate the beneﬁt of CASB generation. A direct control
would apply exactly the same majority-only cleaner after SMOTE.
UGO selects sources using classiﬁer uncertainty, repeatedly reﬁts the classiﬁer, and ﬁlters or relocates generated
observations (cite). It supports diﬀerent generators within the same framework. CASB also separates allocation from
generation, but computes its neighbourhood-based allocation once. Similarity in this organisation does not establish
comparable runtime or predictive performance. We did not include UGO results in the CASB comparison tables.

brute-force search, the required neighbourhoods cost up to 𝑂(𝑛𝑚𝑛𝑝) distance arithmetic, plus backend-dependent

4. Experimental design
4.1. Datasets and evidence populations
The benchmark contains 44 binary tasks from the KEEL collection (Alcalá-Fdez, Sánchez, García, del Jesus,
Ventura, Garrell, Otero, Romero, Bacardit, Rivas, Fernández and Herrera, 2009). Each task supplies ﬁve train/test
pairs. Two seeds, 42 and 137, control inner partitions and stochastic resampling; they do not create ten independent
datasets. Forty tasks have complete records for all nine current methods and four classiﬁers. ADASYN fails on some
repetitions of ecoli-0-1-3-7_vs_2-6, iris0, shuttle-c0-vs-c4 and shuttle-c2-vs-c4. The counts are respectively 1, 5, 4 and
3 per classiﬁer. No replacement sampler is substituted.
The four-classiﬁer benchmark contains 44 × 5 × 2 × 9 × 4 = 15,840 attempted outer evaluations: 15,788 succeeded
and 52 failed. The earlier SVM/KNN-only guide reported 7,920 attempts and 26 failures, which remain valid for that
subset. Including auxiliary Gaussian naive Bayes runs and compatible historical RUS/SMOTE-ENN results brings the
statistical archive to 21,560 attempts and 65 failures. These archive totals describe a broader collection than the current
nine-method benchmark.
Several tasks share an original dataset. Glass, Yeast, Ecoli and Vehicle variants, for example, are not independent
acquisition studies. The sensitivity analysis groups the tasks into 14 source families, or 12 for complete ADASYN
comparisons. All 44 tasks have contributed to development or analysis, including the 38 added after the initial six;

Twenty seeds, 20260–20279, are used. Base random draws are reused across distances and methods within a seed,
which improves pairing but correlates distance-speciﬁc outcomes.

4.3. Compared methods and classiﬁers
The core comparison includes no resampling, SMOTE, Borderline-SMOTE, ROS, ADASYN and the four CASB
variants. The number of minority neighbours for library interpolation methods is min(5, 𝑛𝑚−1); B-SMOTE uses
borderline-1. The extended SVM/KNN statistical comparison adds compatible RUS and SMOTE-ENN results. Those
controls are unavailable under the RF/LR expanded protocol, and no values are inferred for them. Library components
follow the project implementation (Lemaître, Nogueira and Aridas, 2017; Pedregosa, Varoquaux, Gramfort, Michel,
Thirion, Grisel, Blondel, Prettenhofer, Weiss, Dubourg, Vanderplas, Passos, Cournapeau, Brucher, Perrot and Duch-
esnay, 2011).
SVM uses an RBF kernel, 𝐶= 1 and gamma=scale. KNN uses distance weighting and seven neighbours, reduced
only when fewer than seven training observations remain. RF uses 100 trees and minimum leaf size two. LR uses its
implemented default regularization with a maximum of 3,000 iterations. None of these four uses class weights in the
reported current comparison. Classiﬁer hyperparameters and prediction thresholds are not searched.
We retained RF and LR in the main presentation after inspecting their results. Gaussian naive Bayes was also
evaluated and is reported as an auxiliary result. This choice makes the four-classiﬁer presentation exploratory. A
conﬁrmatory study would need to specify its classiﬁer set before inspecting outcomes.
4.4. Application protocol and metrics
Each outer training fold is divided into an inner training set and a stratiﬁed 25% validation set. Every sampler tries
ratios .50, .75 and 1.00. We select the ratio that maximizes (𝐺-mean+𝐹1)∕2 on validation data, preferring the smaller
ratio when scores tie. This objective is used only for ratio selection; the reported outcomes remain separate metrics.
Imputation and scaling are reﬁtted for every inner ﬁt. We then reﬁt the selected pipeline on the full outer training fold
and evaluate it on untouched outer test data.
The two Abalone tasks include a categorical Sex attribute. We apply most-frequent imputation and dense one-hot
encoding ﬁtted on training data, ignoring unknown test categories. Numeric features use the usual numeric pipeline.

4.5. Aggregation, family summaries and statistical procedure
For dataset 𝑑, classiﬁer 𝑐, method 𝑎and metric 𝑚, let 𝑆𝑑𝑐𝑎𝑚be the mean of its ten outer evaluations. An incomplete
group is withheld. Collection-wide averages are 𝐴𝑐𝑎𝑚= |𝐷|−1 ∑
𝑑𝑆𝑑𝑐𝑎𝑚. Combined tables average classiﬁers equally
after averaging datasets; a large dataset does not receive more weight.
We report two retrospective summaries. The collection envelope, max𝑎∈𝐴𝑐𝑎𝑚, chooses one family member for an
entire metric column. Dataset-wise family wins count max𝑎∈𝑆𝑑𝑐𝑎𝑚> max𝑏∈𝑆𝑑𝑐𝑏𝑚and can select a diﬀerent variant
for each dataset. Both use observed outcomes and therefore cannot serve as deployable selectors or conﬁrmatory paired
tests. A joint G-mean/F1 win requires the same variant to exceed every baseline on both metrics. Ties use tolerance
10−12.
Fixed-variant inference uses two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank tests on paired dataset means, following the multiple-
dataset comparison framework of Demšar (2006). Pairs without ADASYN use 44 datasets; ADASYN pairs use 40.
Zero diﬀerences are omitted; diﬀerences are rounded to twelve decimals for ranking and the tie-corrected asymptotic
calculation is used. Symmetry of the paired-diﬀerence distribution and dependence among tasks limit interpretation.
Holm correction (Holm, 1979) spans four variants, all compatible baselines and four metrics separately within each
classiﬁer: 112 tests for SVM/KNN and 80 for RF/LR.
Friedman tests use the strict 40-task cohort, with eleven methods for SVM/KNN and nine for RF/LR. We apply
Holm correction to the four omnibus tests within each classiﬁer; post-hoc all-pairs comparisons form a separate
correction family. Sensitivity analyses use source-family mean diﬀerences and one lexicographically selected task
per family. We also retain exact sign tests. These analyses examine how conclusions change with the test and unit of
comparison, but cannot undo classiﬁer selection or reuse of development data.

5.3. Mechanistic interpretation of metric-speciﬁc performance tradeoﬀs
5.3.1. Conﬁrmed implementation mechanisms
The variants change distinct parts of the training procedure. Original CASB limits displacement in capped
branches; relaxed changes both quotas and displacement; edited adds majority-only removal; and CASB-ROS changes
multiplicities without adding coordinates. These are implementation properties, independent of any measured gain.
Original CASB does not ﬁlter noise, and edited does not clean minority labels.
More minority predictions may reduce 𝐹𝑁while increasing 𝐹𝑃. G-mean improves only when the recall–
speciﬁcity product increases; majority deletion provides no monotonic improvement guarantee.

5.3.3. Plausible explanations and their limits
Restricting generation can concentrate additions near their sources and may help preserve precision by limiting
their inﬂuence farther away. This could explain original CASB’s relative precision/F1 strength, but the current results
do not establish causation or show that original CASB always has fewer false positives than SMOTE. Testing that
explanation requires a cap-only ablation with the same quotas and ratio.
KNN depends on nearby labels and distances: copies can change neighbourhood composition, while new
coordinates can change which points are nearest. For an RBF SVM, duplicate weighting and interpolation can aﬀect
kernel similarities and the regularized margin diﬀerently. RF responds through its splits and leaf class proportions,
and LR through its ﬁtted linear score and empirical loss. These model properties make classiﬁer-dependent eﬀects
plausible. The benchmark does not establish a general causal relationship between each source type and classiﬁer.

5.5. Discussion, ablation needs and threats to validity
The variants compare useful conﬁgurations, but do not form a complete factorial ablation. Original versus relaxed
changes both quotas and caps. Edited versus relaxed can change the selected ratio as well as add cleaning. CASB
versus CASB-ROS changes coordinates and may also change the selected ratio. A controlled study should ﬁx ratios
and source draws while varying coverage-only, border and rare allocation, caps and cleaning separately. The most
immediate missing control is SMOTE followed by the same majority-only three-neighbour mode cleaner.
We did not exhaustively assess neighbourhood size, overlap thresholds, the sparsity quantile or margins. Their
eﬀects may depend on dimensionality, scaling, duplicates and minority sample size. In particular, third-neighbour
distance may assign similar sparsity to a legitimate minority component and a mislabeled observation. Support and
positive-distance checks restrict some branches, but leave the all-minority pool unprotected. A label-noise study would
need declared training-label corruption rates and clean independent test data; the current overlap experiment does not
provide that evidence.
The benchmark reuses development data and contains related tasks evaluated with supplied folds and two seeds.
Identical feature vectors may occur across supplied partitions, so a group-disjoint sensitivity analysis is still needed.

Repeated seeds describe stochastic variation rather than new populations. The lack of corrected source-family Wilcoxon
ﬁndings limits the current evidence and motivates testing on independent data; it does not imply that a future test will
become signiﬁcant.
The baseline set limits the scope of the comparison. The results concern familiar resamplers and do not establish
superiority over Safe-Level-SMOTE, Geometric SMOTE, LNE, density-aware methods or UGO, which were not
benchmarked. Compatible historical RUS/SMOTE-ENN results extend the SVM/KNN comparison, but matched
RF/LR controls are unavailable. Further methods should receive the same preprocessing, classiﬁer settings and
selection budgets.
Test-selected family envelopes are optimistic, and training-only selection can still overﬁt validation (Cawley and
Talbot, 2010). The next study should freeze the procedure, ratio search and classiﬁer set before evaluating unseen
source families.
6. Conclusion and future work
CASB distributes additions across all minority observations and supported borderline and sparse sources. Its four
variants use conditionally capped interpolation, unrestricted interpolation, majority-edited interpolation and guided
replication. The speciﬁed rules and audits make the procedure reproducible, while the displacement bound oﬀers only
a geometric constraint, not a label or generalization guarantee.
The forty-task benchmark shows beneﬁts that depend on the classiﬁer and metric. For RF, the retrospective family
wins on G-mean in 28 tasks and F1 in 26 against the four core resamplers, with twenty same-variant joint wins. Its
best mean F1 remains slightly below SMOTE. Some ﬁxed-variant gains survive correction, but precision losses and
the absence of corrected source-family Wilcoxon ﬁndings limit the case for broader superiority. The simulation and
projected examples explain aspects of generation without establishing robustness to label noise.
Further evaluation should use matched component controls, direct comparisons with close predecessors, categorical-
aware generation, and a frozen selection procedure tested on new source families. The present contribution is an
incremental resampling design with reproducible rules and measured tradeoﬀs.
Data, code and research transparency
The editable project includes ﬁgures, generated tables, complete statistical CSVs and input hashes. The workspace
retains all 44 executed notebooks and the shared implementation. Current results supersede the older two-classiﬁer
guide. Public archive location, author contributions, funding and competing-interest declarations require author
conﬁrmation.
